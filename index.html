Resolve and Resultant Force

Every force can be broken into components along chosen axes, and several forces acting on the same point can be combined into a single equivalent force — the resultant. Both ideas rest on the same trigonometry.

1. Resolving a force into components

A force F acting at angle θ from the positive x-axis (measured counter-clockwise, same convention used throughout this site) has two perpendicular components:

Fx = F cos θ    Fy = F sin θ
Example: F = 50 N at 40°

Fx = 50 × cos 40° = 38.30 N

Fy = 50 × sin 40° = 32.14 N

The dashed lines are the "shadows" of F on each axis — exactly what the Force Calculator's diagrams show for every row. The arc marks θ, swept counter-clockwise from the positive x-axis, same as everywhere else on this site.

Example: F = 50 N at 130°

This force is 50 N at 130° (measured the usual way, from the positive x-axis) — but for hand calculation it's often quicker to use the basic angle: the acute angle between the force and the nearest x-axis, always between 0° and 90°. Here that's the negative x-axis, so the basic angle is 180° − 130° = 50°. Use it to find the size of each component, then fix the sign just by looking at which way the force actually points — no need to carry an angle past 90° through cos/sin at all:

Fx: 50 × cos 50° = 32.14 N, but the force points left, so Fx = −32.14 N

Fy: 50 × sin 50° = 38.30 N, and the force points up, so Fy = +38.30 N

Same magnitude as the first example, just pointing into the second quadrant instead — that's the whole reason Fx flips sign. A negative component simply means the force points along the negative-axis direction; it isn't an error, and it's the same sign convention used for every calculator on this site.

Choosing different axes: an inclined body

The axes don't have to be the "true" horizontal and vertical — any pair of perpendicular directions works, and picking the right pair can make the trigonometry much simpler. Consider a block resting on a 30° incline, pulled by a rope with force P = 100 N directed 20° above the incline surface. The same force can be resolved either way:

Horizontal / Vertical
Parallel / Perpendicular to incline
Component Horizontal / Vertical Parallel / Perpendicular
Px / P∥ Px = 100 cos 50° = 64.28 N P∥ = 100 cos 20° = 93.97 N
Py / P⊥ Py = 100 sin 50° = 76.60 N P⊥ = 100 sin 20° = 34.20 N

Same force, same physical direction — only the reference axes changed. Against true horizontal/vertical, P's angle first needs the incline's 30° added to its own 20° to get 50° before cos/sin can be applied. Against the incline's own axes, the 20° given in the problem can be used directly. Neither frame is "more correct" than the other, but matching the axes to the geometry of the problem often means less setup.

2. Finding the resultant of several forces

To combine several forces acting at the same point, resolve every force into x/y components using the same sign convention, add the components separately, then convert the sum back into a magnitude and angle:

ΣFx, ΣFy  →  FR = √(ΣFx² + ΣFy²)    θ = tan−1(ΣFyΣFx)

Sign convention (same as every calculator on this site): rightward is +x, upward is +y, and angles are measured counter-clockwise from the positive x-axis. A force pointing left or down simply has a negative component — it isn't a special case, just the same formula with θ in a different quadrant. The example below uses four forces, one in each quadrant, to show every sign combination.

F325 N200° (basic 20°)−23.49 N−8.55 N
F420 N300° (basic 60°)+10.00 N−17.32 N

Moment

A moment is the turning effect a force has about a point — how much it makes something rotate, and which way. It depends on both the size of the force and how far (and in what direction) it acts from that point.

1. What is a moment?

Think of turning a bolt with a wrench: push on the handle, and the bolt rotates about its own axis. That rotational effect is the moment (also called torque) of the force about that pivot point. Push harder, or push further from the bolt, and the turning effect gets stronger — exactly what the formula in the next section captures.

Sign convention (same as every calculator on this site): a moment that would rotate the body counter-clockwise (CCW) is positive; one that would rotate it clockwise (CW) is negative. This is just a labelling convention — a "negative" moment isn't smaller or weaker, it just turns the other way.

2. Calculating a moment

In general, a force doesn't have to act right at the pivot, or even point straight at it — it just needs a line of action (the straight line the force acts along, extended in both directions). The moment of the force about a point is that force multiplied by the perpendicular distance from the pivot to that line:

M = F × d

where d is measured along the shortest (perpendicular) path from the pivot to the line of action — not along the force itself, unless that happens to be the shortest path. The two cases below are just two ways of finding that distance.

2a. Force perpendicular to the pivot: M = F × d

This is the simplest case of the general formula above: the force is already applied perpendicular to the handle, so the given length is already the perpendicular distance d, and the moment is simply:

M = F × d

where d is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force.

Example: wrench on a bolt

A wrench handle of length d = 0.25 m extends from the bolt, and a force F = 60 N is applied straight down at its end — perpendicular to the handle.

M = 60 × 0.25 = 15.00 N·m

Pushing down on a handle that extends to the right rotates it clockwise, so by the sign convention above this moment is −15.00 N·m (reported as 15.00 N·m CW).

2b. Force at an angle to the handle

Often the force isn't applied perpendicular to the arm. Take the same wrench and handle as above (d = 0.25 m), but now the force is applied at the end of the handle at an angle instead of straight down — F = 80 N at 60° to the handle, angled so it swings the handle the other way this time.

Extend the Line of Action
Method 1

Draw the force's line of action as a straight line through where it acts, extended until a perpendicular from the pivot can reach it, then drop that perpendicular — its length is d⊥. The 60° angle between the handle and the (extended) line of action gives a right triangle with hypotenuse d and opposite side d⊥.

d⊥ = d sin 60° = 0.25 × 0.8660 ≈ 0.217 m

M = F × d⊥ = 80 × 0.217 ≈ 17.32 N·m

Resolve into Components
Method 2

Split F into a component along the handle (F∥ = F cos 60° = 40.00 N) and a component perpendicular to it (F⊥ = F sin 60° ≈ 69.28 N), then find each component's own moment and add them. F∥ acts along the handle itself, which already passes straight through the pivot, so its perpendicular distance — and its moment — is zero.

M = F⊥ × d = 69.28 × 0.25 ≈ 17.32 N·m

Total: 0 + 17.32 = 17.32 N·m

Both methods agree: M ≈ +17.32 N·m (CCW) — the force swings the handle the opposite way from the 2a example. Use whichever is more convenient for the geometry in front of you; they'll always give the same answer.

3. Net moment from several forces

Several forces acting on the same body each produce their own moment about a chosen pivot; the net moment is just their sum, using the same CCW-positive sign convention:

ΣM = M1 + M2 + M3 + …

The example below uses three forces about the same pivot at the origin, with deliberately mixed CCW/CW contributions so the net result shows real cancellation rather than everything agreeing. Each force here happens to be exactly horizontal or vertical, so — just like 2a — the perpendicular distance d from the pivot to its line of action is simply the other coordinate of where it acts (F1 is vertical, so d is its x-position; F2 and F3 are horizontal, so d is the size of their y-position), and the moment is just F × d:

Force Magnitude Angle Position d Moment (F × d)
F140 N90°(0.5, 0) m0.5 m40 × 0.5 = +20.00 N·m
F230 N0°(0, 0.4) m0.4 m30 × 0.4 = −12.00 N·m
F325 N180°(−0.3, −0.2) m0.2 m25 × 0.2 = −5.00 N·m
Σ (sum)20.00 − 12.00 − 5.00 = +3.00 N·m

ΣM = 20.00 − 12.00 − 5.00 = +3.00 N·m, i.e. a small net counter-clockwise rotation — the larger CCW contribution from F1 nearly, but not quite, cancels the two CW contributions from F2 and F3. Try the same forces in the to see it computed live.
